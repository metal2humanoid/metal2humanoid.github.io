Metal2Humanoid

Building a Humanoid Robot from the Ground Up

A hands-on introduction to humanoid robotics—from motors and actuators to coordinated motion and learned behavior. Over four weeks, students work in teams to assemble, program, and test components of an open-source humanoid.

The course platform is derived from Berkeley Humanoid Lite and assembled from actuators that students build in Week 1. Each week combines two lectures, a guided hardware lab, and a scaffolded Python assignment.

What students will learn

  1. Week 1

    Motor → to Actuator

    How motors, transmissions, encoders, and feedback control combine to produce controlled joint motion. Students assemble and test an actuator module from prepared components and implement motor position control.

  2. Week 2

    Actuator → to Motion

    Robot modeling, forward and inverse kinematics, and trajectory generation. Teams assemble actuators into a limb, validate joint motion, and implement end-effector motion control in simulation.

  3. Week 3

    Motion → to Learning

    Reinforcement learning for locomotion and the challenges of transferring policies from simulation to hardware. Teams integrate limbs into a lower-body assembly for supported tests and train a locomotion policy in a scaffolded simulation environment.

  4. Week 4

    Learning → to Humanoid

    Skill representations, sequencing, and integrated behavior. Teams complete system integration, implement a simple skill-sequencing task in simulation, and present a demonstration with a brief technical report.

How the course works

Four weeks is enough because the course is a guided tour of the whole stack, not a deep dive into any one field. Each week's lectures cover only the concepts needed for that week's lab, hardware arrives as prepared kits, and software comes as fill-in-the-blank scaffolds. Students leave with a working understanding of how a humanoid comes together and one subsystem they built and tested themselves.

Hardware components and software scaffolds are prepared in advance. Students work in teams on shared builds using prepared hardware kits, guided labs, and scaffolded Python exercises. Labs focus on assembly, wiring, testing, and integration rather than fabrication, which is what makes a 90-minute weekly lab feasible.

Activities include four weekly assignments, laboratory participation, and a final team demonstration and report. Instructors give feedback on understanding, testing, and documentation. No academic credit or formal grade is awarded.

Outcomes and expectations

The course ends with a team demonstration and a brief technical report documenting hardware and software results.

Successful untethered walking and manipulation are stretch goals, not promised outcomes. Final demonstrations will reflect validated hardware capabilities. Simulation-based activities provide a parallel route for learning and evaluation whenever hardware needs troubleshooting.

Builds are shared team builds. Students do not each build, or take home, a complete robot.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how motors, transmissions, encoders, and feedback control produce controlled joint motion.
  • Model robot limbs and implement basic end-effector motion in simulation.
  • Train and evaluate a scaffolded locomotion policy and identify simulation-to-reality challenges.
  • Integrate and test robot subsystems, document results, and demonstrate coordinated behavior.

Hardware platform

The course platform is derived from Berkeley Humanoid Lite, an open-source humanoid from the Hybrid Robotics group at UC Berkeley with modular 3D-printed gearboxes and widely available components. The course build keeps its structure while HBRN contributors adapt the actuator design and selected components; final choices depend on the hardware partners confirmed before January. Berkeley Humanoid Lite's software is released under the MIT License and its hardware files under CC BY-SA 4.0.

  2. Demonstrated Simulation October 2026

    Simulation: walking policy on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model

    A reinforcement-learning walking policy (PPO) trained in MuJoCo on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model, following a forward velocity command. The policy outputs joint-position targets at 50 Hz to a PD controller, the same interface a real motor driver exposes. Simulation only; no hardware result is implied.

    Simulation and video by Willie McClinton. Robot model: Berkeley Humanoid Lite (Hybrid Robotics, UC Berkeley), CC BY-SA 4.0.

  3. Demonstrated Simulation October 2026

    Simulation: standing policy on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model

    A PPO standing policy trained in MuJoCo on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model, using only observations a real robot can measure: IMU angular velocity, gravity direction, joint positions and velocities, and the previous action. This is the scaffolded setup the Week 3 assignment builds on. Simulation only.

    Simulation and video by Willie McClinton. Robot model: Berkeley Humanoid Lite (Hybrid Robotics, UC Berkeley), CC BY-SA 4.0.

About the Homebrew Robotics Network

Metal2Humanoid is organized by its instructors together with members of the Homebrew Robotics Network (HBRN), the builder community behind the initiative.

The Homebrew Robotics Network is a community of builders working toward open, accessible, reproducible robotics: hardware that can be built from widely available parts, documented well enough for others to repeat, and shared under open licenses. For Metal2Humanoid, HBRN hardware contributors lead the robot design and the Week 1 actuator materials, and the instructors lead the simulation, locomotion, and task weeks.

What comes next

The immediate next step is a larger open humanoid built around commercial actuators, now in design by HBRN contributors as the platform that follows the course build. It is not a course deliverable. Further out, HBRN is exploring two companion efforts that may extend the course materials: OSH Atlas, an atlas of open humanoid projects and resources, and UMOJA, a framework for assessing and comparing open humanoid designs, both possible future extensions for documentation, resource sharing, and benchmarking.
