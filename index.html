Metal2Humanoid

Building a Humanoid Robot from the Ground Up

A hands-on introduction to humanoid robotics—from motors and actuators to coordinated motion and learned behavior. Over four weeks, students work in teams to assemble, program, and test components of an open-source humanoid.

Every student builds an actuator in Week 1 on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite platform. Teams then assemble, commission, and program the Metal2Humanoid, a larger custom humanoid, and the course ends with a public showcase of both robots on January 29.

What students will learn

  1. Week 1 · Earth

    Motor → to Actuator

    BLDC motors, drivers, cycloidal gearboxes, encoders, and PID control. Every student assembles, calibrates, and tests their own actuator; by Friday, selected student-built actuators drive a prepared walking mechanism.

  2. Week 2 · Water

    Actuator → to Motion

    Kinematics, dynamics, balance, and humanoid architecture. Six teams assemble the Metal2Humanoid (legs, arms, torso, and integration), commission it, and run supported standing and walking tests, targeting walking by Friday.

  3. Week 3 · Fire

    Motion → to Learning

    Reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and sim-to-real transfer. Teams train or fine-tune policies, develop one robot skill each, deploy feasible skills to hardware, and build a shared skill library.

  4. Week 4 · Air

    Learning → to Humanoid

    Task planning and skill sequencing. Teams integrate skills into coordinated behaviors, rehearse, and present both humanoids at the public showcase on January 29.

How the course works

Four weeks is enough because the course is a guided tour of the whole stack, not a deep dive into any one field. Each session's lecture covers only the concepts needed for that session's hands-on work, hardware arrives as prepared kits with prefabricated parts and preflashed electronics, and software comes as fill-in-the-blank scaffolds with pretrained policies.

Each roughly three-hour session combines 20–40 minutes of lecture and demonstration, 90–120 minutes of guided hands-on work, and the remaining time for testing, debugging, and discussion. All sessions take place in the same lab, with additional open lab time for assembly, testing, and integration.

Students work in six teams of two to four. Every student completes an individual actuator; teams share responsibility for humanoid assembly, software development, and testing, and responsibilities may shift as the course progresses. Activities include four weekly assignments, lab participation, and the final showcase. No academic credit or formal grade is awarded.

Outcomes and expectations

The course ends with a public showcase on January 29 featuring both humanoid platforms: student-built actuators, Berkeley Humanoid Lite walking and skills, the Metal2Humanoid walking and skills, and six team presentations with live or recorded demonstrations and lessons learned.

Milestones are targets, not promises. Assembling the full Metal2Humanoid and getting it walking by the end of Week 2 is ambitious; walking validation may extend into Week 3, and live demonstrations at the showcase depend on robot readiness and safety approval. Simulation and pretrained policies keep the software curriculum moving regardless of hardware delays, and every team prepares a recorded or simulated backup.

Builds are shared team builds. Each student keeps the experience of building and testing an actuator; the robots and kits stay with the course for future offerings and community builds.

Course goals

  1. Individually assemble, calibrate, and test a functional actuator.
  2. Demonstrate coordinated actuator control through a prepared Berkeley Humanoid Lite by the end of Week 1.
  3. Assemble and commission the full Metal2Humanoid, targeting walking by the end of Week 2.
  4. Develop and evaluate locomotion, manipulation, and other robot skills.
  5. Integrate learned and programmed skills into coordinated robot behaviors.
  6. Present the work at a public final showcase featuring both humanoid platforms.

Two platforms

Week 1 uses Berkeley Humanoid Lite, an open-source humanoid from the Hybrid Robotics group at UC Berkeley with modular 3D-printed cycloidal actuators, as the actuator and introductory platform: every student builds a BHL-style actuator, and a prepared BHL-like mechanism walks on student-built actuators by Friday. From Week 2 the course moves to the Metal2Humanoid, a larger custom humanoid (provisionally 26 actuated joints) assembled and commissioned by the six teams. Its CAD and actuator selection are being finalized before January. Berkeley Humanoid Lite's software is MIT-licensed and its hardware files are CC BY-SA 4.0.

  2. Demonstrated Simulation October 2026

    Simulation: walking policy on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model

    A reinforcement-learning walking policy (PPO) trained in MuJoCo on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model, following a forward velocity command. The policy outputs joint-position targets at 50 Hz to a PD controller, the same interface a real motor driver exposes. Simulation only; no hardware result is implied.

    Simulation and video by Willie McClinton. Robot model: Berkeley Humanoid Lite (Hybrid Robotics, UC Berkeley), CC BY-SA 4.0.

  3. Demonstrated Simulation October 2026

    Simulation: standing policy on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model

    A PPO standing policy trained in MuJoCo on the Berkeley Humanoid Lite model, using only observations a real robot can measure: IMU angular velocity, gravity direction, joint positions and velocities, and the previous action. This is the scaffolded setup the Week 3 assignment builds on. Simulation only.

    Simulation and video by Willie McClinton. Robot model: Berkeley Humanoid Lite (Hybrid Robotics, UC Berkeley), CC BY-SA 4.0.

About the Homebrew Robotics Network

Metal2Humanoid is organized by its instructors together with members of the Homebrew Robotics Network (HBRN), the builder community behind the initiative.

The Homebrew Robotics Network is a community of builders working toward open, accessible, reproducible robotics: hardware that can be built from widely available parts, documented well enough for others to repeat, and shared under open licenses. For Metal2Humanoid, HBRN hardware contributors lead the robot design and the Week 1 actuator materials, and the instructors lead the simulation, locomotion, and task weeks.

What comes next

The Metal2Humanoid built in the course, a larger custom humanoid around commercial actuators, is released openly afterwards, together with the lab guides and notebooks, so that other groups can repeat the course. Further out, HBRN is exploring two companion efforts that may extend the materials: OSH Atlas, an atlas of open humanoid projects and resources, and UMOJA, a framework for assessing and comparing open humanoid designs, both possible future extensions for documentation, resource sharing, and benchmarking.
