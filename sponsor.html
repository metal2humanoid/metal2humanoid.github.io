How to sponsor·Metal2Humanoid, proposed MIT IAP 2027

Help students build humanoid robots from the ground up.

Metal2Humanoid is a proposed non-credit MIT IAP 2027 course in which about twenty students work in teams to build actuators, assemble them into a humanoid platform derived from Berkeley Humanoid Lite, and program it. The course requests no departmental funding: the organizing team is arranging hardware and materials through sponsors and parts donors, and the adapted design and course materials are released openly.

What support makes possible

  • Reusable hardware

    Actuator modules, limbs, and shared humanoid builds that are used across the four labs and kept for future offerings and community builds.

  • Prepared lab kits

    Pre-fabricated and pre-sourced components, spares, and tools, so that 90-minute labs can focus on assembly, wiring, and testing rather than fabrication.

  • Documented learning resources

    Lab guides, notebooks, and build notes published openly so that other groups can repeat the course.

What we need

In-kind hardware is the most useful form of support: actuators and motors above all, then compute, printers and fabrication, tools and test equipment. Financial support covers what in-kind contributions do not, such as kit preparation, consumables, spares, and shipping.

  1. Actuators, motors, encoders, and sensors

    Joint actuators or motor-plus-gearbox modules, magnetic encoders, IMUs, and load sensing for the lab builds and spares.

  2. Batteries, electronics, compute, and motor controllers

    Battery packs and management, power distribution, motor controller boards, CAN interfaces, and on-board or bench compute.

  3. Fabrication, machining, printing, and materials

    3D printing capacity and filament, laser-cut or machined interface parts, fasteners, bearings, and structural stock for kit preparation.

  4. Tools, test equipment, spare parts, and consumables

    Bench power supplies, multimeters, soldering stations, hand tools, robot support fixtures, cabling, and connectors.

  5. Financial support

    Funds for components, kit preparation, and replacement parts, used where in-kind contributions do not cover a need.

  6. Technical mentorship and engineering support

    Review of designs and lab procedures, help with motor control and firmware, and occasional guest participation in labs.

Where support goes

A small, non-credit course still needs real hardware. Here is what contributions are used for.

  • Actuator kits for every student: the Week 1 build, kept afterwards as reusable teaching modules.
  • Limb and lower-body assemblies for the team builds, plus one or two complete course robots and spares.
  • Compute for simulation and on-robot control, batteries, motor controllers, and sensors.
  • Kit preparation: printing, fasteners, bearings, cabling, test fixtures, and shipping between build sites.
  • Documentation and open release of the adapted design and the course materials.

What sponsors receive

We keep recognition straightforward and tied to real use of what you provide.

  • Recognition on this website and in relevant project materials.
  • Credited hardware demonstrations and build updates that show your components in use.
  • Opportunities to engage with student and independent robotics builders.
  • Practical feedback from hands-on use of your products in a teaching setting.
  • Participation in a growing open robotics education community.

How Big Companies Fit In

Open robotics education and the companies that make robotics hardware need each other. The relationship works best when each side contributes what it is good at.

Companies contribute

  • Components and development kits
  • Manufacturing and design-for-manufacturing expertise
  • Tools, test equipment, and compute
  • Engineering time and resources

The community contributes

  • Experimentation with those technologies in real builds
  • Open documentation that others can follow
  • Honest, practical feedback from hands-on use
  • People who learn to build with those technologies

For an actuator or motor manufacturer, the partnership can go further: the course platform and its open design can be built around your actuators, with published assembly guides, test data, and student builds that use them, and direct feedback from a cohort learning on your hardware.
